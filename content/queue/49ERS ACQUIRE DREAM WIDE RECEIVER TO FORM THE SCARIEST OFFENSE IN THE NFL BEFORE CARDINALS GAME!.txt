Story number one. The Miami game was not just a win. It was a statement.

San Francisco was expected to win by multiple scores. They didn't just cover that, they blew right past it, winning by 22. Through two weeks, the Niners have outscored their opponents 62 to 20. That's a plus-42 point differential after two games. Miami actually tried to play keep-away in the first half, running the ball and eating clock, and it kept the score close for a while. But look at what happened when the field got short. The 49ers went five for five in the red zone. Five trips, five touchdowns. Miami got down there twice in the first half and came away with two field goals. That's the whole game right there. One team finishes. The other team settles.

And here's a stat that tells you how smooth this offense was operating. San Francisco ran 49 plays and faced only seven third downs. Seven! That means the Niners were picking up yards on first and second down so consistently that they almost never had to sweat the money down. It looked like a playoff team playing a rebuilding team, and that's exactly what it was.

Now let's talk Brock Purdy, because the box score, as crazy as it was, doesn't tell the whole story. Yes, he was 20 of 22. Yes, he had a 149.1 passer rating. But look at how he got there. He rushed for 30 yards, and 28 of them came on a single drive, turning broken plays into two first downs. Over and over, he bought time in the pocket, let his receivers uncover, and then delivered the ball down the field. That's not a checkdown quarterback padding his completion percentage. That's a guy hunting explosive plays.

And the charting numbers back it up. On late downs, Purdy converted 83 percent of his throws into either a first down or a touchdown. His explosive play rate was 24 percent. Almost one in every four plays went for a chunk gain. When Miami sent the blitz, he was 6 of 8 for 121 yards and two touchdowns. Think about that. Defenses blitz to rattle the quarterback. Against Purdy, the blitz became a gift. In the third quarter alone, he hit three completions of 20 yards or more. That's the quarter where this game went from close to over.

And credit Kyle Shanahan here too. He called a game that asked Purdy to push the ball down the field, and Purdy answered every single time.

Now here's the history. That was the eighth game of Purdy's career with a passer rating of 140 or higher. That ties him with Joe Montana for the second most in 49ers history. Joe Montana! The only name ahead of him is Steve Young, with 13. So Purdy needs five more to tie a Hall of Famer who owns a Super Bowl MVP. And remember, Purdy already has the most 140-plus games of any quarterback in his first five seasons in league history. This kid isn't just playing well. He's climbing a franchise mountain that only two legends have ever stood on.

Now, George Kittle. We talked about his first touchdown since the Achilles tear, but there's more. Kittle hit 600 career receptions against Miami, and he got there in 126 games. That makes him the third-fastest tight end in NFL history to reach that number. The only two faster? Travis Kelce at 110 games, and Jason Witten at 125. That's the company he's keeping. And he's doing it at 32 years old, about eight months after tearing his Achilles in the playoff win over Philadelphia back in January.

And one play in particular showed you exactly why he's special. The 49ers ran a leak concept, and Miami's defense recognized it. They were sitting on it. Most tight ends run the route as drawn, and the ball falls incomplete. Kittle didn't. He read the defense on the fly, adjusted his route, broke inside, and turned a likely incompletion into a 26-yard catch. That's not athleticism. That's veteran football intelligence. He finished with 80 yards, added 30 of them after the catch, topped 17 miles per hour on the GPS tracker, forced a missed tackle, and caught his only contested target. The Achilles questions? Answered.

This is Ketuil NFL Stories, and now let's talk about the man who might be the most underrated story of this whole season. Raheem Morris.

Here's what fascinates me. The defense that beat Miami looked nothing like the defense that beat the Rams. Against Los Angeles, Morris played it conservative. He blitzed Matthew Stafford only four times the entire game, and he lived in a two-high safety shell, keeping everything in front. Then against Miami, he flipped the script. He blitzed 39.3 percent of the time. He went to single-high safety looks on nearly 70 percent of snaps. He used five-man fronts with an extra defensive tackle on the field instead of a linebacker to shut down Miami's run game. But here's the genius part: even with all that pressure, the Niners still played zone coverage about 90 percent of the time. So the quarterback feels the heat, but when he looks downfield, there's no man-to-man matchup to exploit. Just bodies in windows.

The result? San Francisco had the highest sack percentage in the entire league in Week 2, at 14.3 percent. Four sacks on a mobile quarterback. That's not luck. That's a coordinator who studies the opponent, looks at his own personnel, and builds a plan for that week. He is not married to one scheme. And trust me, you're going to see in a minute why that matters so much right now.

Let's talk about the young guys, because they're making this defense go. Rookie defensive tackle Gracen Halton should have had a sack on the very first play of the game. He posted a 21.4 percent pressure rate, eighth among all defensive tackles with at least 10 pass-rushing snaps. And his run defense was even better. He ranked fourth among all defensive tackles in run-stop percentage. There was one play where he controlled his blocker, looked one way, disengaged, and still made the tackle. Right now, Halton is playing like a first-round pick.

Ji'Ayir Brown had a shaky first snap, but after that, he was a menace against the run. Three run stops, third most among all safeties that week, and he didn't miss a single tackle. Marques Sigle looked more confident too, jumping into passing lanes and showing real juice as a blitzer. He got home for a sack. And Upton Stout? Yes, he was on the field for that late touchdown, but that came with the score at 35 to 6 and under three minutes left. Outside of that, he allowed just 39 yards on six targets and topped 20 miles per hour on the GPS tracker, the second-fastest speed on the team that week. He picked up a tackle for loss and a pass breakup. That's a young corner growing up in real time.

And we can't skip the offensive line. Trent Williams, at 38 years old, didn't allow a single pressure on 25 pass-blocking snaps, even with Miami throwing six different defenders at him. Jake Brendel and Dominick Puni both posted clean sheets. Connor Colby allowed only one pressure and made a couple of impact plays, and Colton McKivitz held up on the right side. When your quarterback is pushing the ball downfield this aggressively and only throws two incompletions, the big guys up front deserve their flowers. The real test is coming, though. The Denver and Seattle defensive fronts will be a whole different animal.

And let's zoom out. This is the fifth time since 2019 that the Niners have started 2-0. The other four? 2019, 2021, 2023, and 2025. They made the playoffs every single time. They scored touchdowns on their first five possessions against Miami for the first time since at least 1981. And they've done all of it while missing key pieces at receiver. Nobody should be guaranteeing a Super Bowl in September. But the signs are real.

Which brings us to story number two. Because those injuries at receiver forced the front office to act.

The 49ers have signed veteran wide receiver Brandin Cooks to their practice squad. And happy birthday to him, by the way, he just turned 33 on September 25th. This is his 13th season in the NFL. Last year, he played 15 games between New Orleans and Buffalo and caught 24 passes for 279 yards. That's not a big number. But don't let last season fool you, because the resume is serious.

Cooks is a former first-round pick out of Oregon State. He has six 1,000-yard seasons in his career. Six! He's played for the Saints, the Patriots, the Rams, the Texans, the Cowboys, and the Bills, and now San Francisco becomes his seventh team. And here's a fun storyline: he's just 189 yards away from 10,000 career receiving yards. Imagine him crossing that milestone in red and gold.

Why now? Because this receiver room is running on fumes. Ricky Pearsall is done for the year. Rookie De'Zhaun Stribling is out for an extended stretch. Demarcus Robinson has a high ankle sprain. Christian Kirk is working back from a calf injury. Against Miami, guys from the bottom of the depth chart were forced into real snaps. Shanahan said right after the game that they had to add somebody. Cooks is that somebody.

Now let's be realistic. Nobody expects Cooks to suddenly turn back into the guy who put up 1,000-yard seasons for fun. That's not what San Francisco needs. They need depth. They need experience. They need a pro who knows how to prepare, who's played in different systems for different coaches, including big playoff moments, and who can step in and run the right route at the right depth when Purdy needs him. The plan is for him to get up to speed on the practice squad first, but with this injury list, he could be on the field sooner than anyone planned. Cooks doesn't need to carry this offense. He just needs to fit in. And that's what makes this move smart.

And now, story number three. The one nobody wanted to hear.

Nick Bosa is out for Sunday against Arizona, and he's expected to miss multiple weeks with a calf injury. Here's the frustrating part: it happened in practice on Thursday, during individual warmups. Earlier in the week, he'd been listed with a rest day for his knee. Then on Thursday, he pulled the calf. Shanahan said Bosa could miss around a few weeks. And according to reports, the team is actually leaning against putting him on injured reserve, for a very specific reason. Teams can only bring eight players back from IR during the season, and San Francisco is already burning through those spots. That tells you everything about how brutal this injury situation has become.

And look at this defensive line. Bosa is out. Rookie Romello Height is out after hand surgery. Rookie tackle James Thompson Jr. is out with a high ankle sprain. C.J. West is dealing with a knee injury expected to cost him multiple weeks. The San Francisco Chronicle called the defensive line attrition a horror show, and honestly, that's not an exaggeration.

There is good news, though. Mike Evans was a limited participant in practice on Friday after missing Wednesday and Thursday, and he's officially listed as questionable. That's a real step forward. Christian McCaffrey, Trent Williams, and Dre Greenlaw are all good to go.

And here's where everything connects. Remember what we just said about Raheem Morris? He's not locked into one scheme. He adjusts to the opponent and to the personnel he has available. Two weeks ago, that sounded like a nice coaching compliment. Now, it's the most important trait on this entire staff. Without Bosa, Morris has to manufacture pressure through scheme, with blitzes, with guys like Keion White, Gracen Halton, Marques Sigle, and Fred Warner coming from different angles. And the Miami tape showed us he can do exactly that.
