Recovery between plays takes longer. And by the fourth quarter, the team that handles it better can have a real edge.

The two teams are approaching that challenge in completely opposite ways, and honestly, it's one of the most fascinating subplots of the week. The 49ers didn't even fly home after beating Dallas. They flew straight from Texas to Colorado Springs, where they're practicing all week at the Air Force Academy. The idea is simple: let the players' bodies adapt to high altitude for an entire week before they set foot in Mexico. On Wednesday, the team practiced at half speed while players adjusted. And Shanahan admitted, with a laugh, that he was out of breath just walking over to talk to reporters. The 49ers will fly to Mexico City on Saturday.

If this approach sounds familiar, it should. Back in September, the 49ers opened the season against the Rams in Melbourne, in the first NFL regular season game ever played in Australia. San Francisco arrived early to adjust to the long trip and the time difference. The Rams arrived much later. And the 49ers won comfortably, 27 to 7, with Purdy throwing three touchdown passes. The 49ers also prepared in Colorado Springs before their last game in Mexico City, in 2022, and they beat the Arizona Cardinals 38 to 10 that night. So there's a track record here. Shanahan was careful, though. He said altitude is rarely the difference between winning and losing, but it's definitely nicer to play when you're used to it.

The Vikings, on the other hand, are staying home. They'll fly into Mexico City on Saturday and leave right after the game. Instead of traveling early, they've been training in a high-altitude room at their own facility. According to the team's head of health and performance, Tyler Williams, they've logged roughly 450 sessions in that room, and nearly the entire roster has done at least five sessions, experiencing simulated altitude as high as 12,000 feet. Their reasoning is that keeping the normal weekly routine is more valuable than the extra time at altitude. Head coach Kevin O'Connell said the staff consulted soccer teams, NBA teams and others who've dealt with similar challenges, and that they'll manage tempo and substitutions to compete for all 60 minutes. Brian Flores added that his defense will rotate a lot, and that everybody will have to be ready to play.

So who has the better plan? Honestly, we don't know yet. Simulated altitude and actually playing a full NFL game at more than 7,000 feet are not exactly the same thing. But the Vikings are a professional, data-driven organization, and they clearly took this seriously. If you're a 49ers fan, here's the way to think about it: the altitude might give San Francisco a small edge late in the game. It probably won't decide the game by itself. The football will.

So what exactly does Flores do that's so dangerous? One word: pressure. And not the occasional blitz on third down. Constant, relentless, disguised pressure. To put that in perspective, the league-wide average is around 32%, and that's already one of the highest rates in years. The Vikings are blitzing more than twice as often as a typical defense. On some weeks, they've sent extra rushers on the majority of snaps. And the results speak for themselves. Minnesota leads the NFL with 37 sacks, more than four per game. Andrew Van Ginkel has eight and a half sacks by himself. The Vikings are allowing only 14 points per game, the best mark in the league. They're giving up just 273.3 yards per game, second fewest in the NFL, and only 4.5 yards per play, also second best. Opposing quarterbacks have a passer rating below 70 against them. So how do the 49ers attack this defense? Let's go through it, because this is where it gets really interesting.

First, the running game. One of the quieter storylines about this Vikings defense is that it's not quite as dominant against the run as it is against the pass. Before the Green Bay game, Minnesota had allowed more than 130 rushing yards in three straight games. In Week 8, Detroit's Jahmyr Gibbs ran for 153 yards and a touchdown against them, using a lot of plays where an extra blocker came across the formation to seal the edge. McCaffrey has scored a rushing touchdown in eight straight games. Rookie Kaelon Black has given them a physical second option. If San Francisco can run the ball effectively on first down, it keeps the offense out of the long third downs where Flores loves to dial up his most exotic pressures.

Second, the quick passing game and the screens. When a defense blitzes this often, it's taking a gamble. It sends extra rushers and hopes to get home before the ball comes out. The way to punish that is to get the ball out quickly, to receivers and running backs who can turn short throws into long gains. That's exactly what McCaffrey, Deebo Samuel, George Kittle and Keenan Allen do best. Remember that first drive against Dallas? Run, then screens, then play-action. That's the blueprint. A well-timed screen against an all-out blitz can go for fifty yards, because there's nobody left behind the rushers.

Third, protection, and this is where the 49ers' identity collides with Flores's strategy. According to the numbers, San Francisco sends all five eligible receivers into its passing concepts on about 80% of dropbacks, when you include chip blocks, the highest rate in the league. In other words, the 49ers like to keep the defense stretched with as many receivers as possible. Minnesota, meanwhile, uses its disguises to confuse protection and make offenses pay for sending fewer players to block. So Shanahan has a big decision to make. Does he stick with his usual approach and trust Purdy to get rid of the ball quickly? Or does he keep an extra tight end or running back in to block, giving up a receiver in exchange for a little more time? I'd expect a mix. And Trent Williams will be enormous here. He's been playing at an elite level, even at 38. If he can handle his side of the line on his own, the 49ers can slide their help to the other side.

Fourth, discipline. You can't beat a Flores defense if you keep putting yourself in third-and-long. Pre-snap penalties, holding calls and negative plays are exactly what this defense feeds on. The 49ers were flagged a lot in Dallas, and Shanahan mentioned penalties as an area that needs to improve. Against Minnesota, every lost yard matters twice as much.

And fifth, the altitude could quietly become part of the game plan. Remember what Flores said: his defense will have to rotate a lot to deal with the thin air. If the 49ers can string together long drives, keep the same defenders on the field for eight, ten, twelve plays in a row, and pick up first downs before Minnesota can substitute, those blitzing linebackers and edge rushers may start to lose a step late in drives. A tired pass rusher doesn't get home. A tired linebacker gets a little slower in coverage.
