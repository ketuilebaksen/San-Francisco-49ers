Forty points on the scoreboard usually ends an argument about a defense. In this case, it starts one. The San Francisco 49ers gave up 40 points to the Dallas Cowboys, allowed 439 yards, and watched Dak Prescott throw for 343 yards and five touchdowns as Dallas came storming back in the fourth quarter. By the plain numbers, that looks like a defense in serious trouble. But the underlying numbers tell a very different story. And heading into Sunday night's showdown in Mexico City against the best defense in the league, there's a lot more good news for the 49ers than most people realize. Today we're going to look at three pieces of it. First, why this defense may be much better than it looked in Arlington. Second, why Brock Purdy might be the worst possible quarterback for a blitz-happy Vikings defense to face. And third, how the 49ers' receiver room is about to look very different, with Mike Evans questionable and a promising rookie back on the field for the first time since September. Let's get into it.

Let's start with the defense, because this is the argument that's been dominating the conversation all week. And let me be clear about what I'm arguing. I'm not saying the Cowboys game was good defense. It wasn't. I'm saying the final score hides some important signs about where this unit is headed. A final score rewards the team that finishes stronger, not necessarily the team that played better over 60 minutes. Points allowed are shaped by field position, tempo, penalties and what the offense does before the ball changes hands. That's why a second look at the process, not just the result, can tell you more about the future.

So let's start with the criticism, because it's fair. In their last three games, the 49ers' pass rush hasn't gotten home very often. Against Atlanta, according to ESPN, they pressured Michael Penix Jr. on only 3 of his 34 dropbacks. Against the Raiders, they pressured Kirk Cousins nine times without a sack. And against Dallas, they recorded just one shared sack, split between Mykel Williams and Dre Greenlaw. On top of that, the secondary was a real problem. Renardo Green and Deommodore Lenoir were both banged up all week, barely practicing before the game, and Prescott found holes in the coverage repeatedly as Dallas rallied. If you only look at the sack column and the final score, you'd conclude that the front isn't doing its job.

But now let's look at the numbers that measure what actually happened on each snap. Analyst Kyle Posey pointed out that Nick Bosa had the third-highest pass rush win rate of any edge rusher in the entire NFL in Week 10, among players with at least 50 snaps. Pass rush win rate measures how often a rusher beats his blocker within a short window of time. So it tells you who's winning the matchup, whether or not the quarterback ends up on the ground. And it wasn't just Bosa. Bosa, Keion White and Mykel Williams all ranked in the top 21 among edge rushers that week. Three players from one team in the top 21. That's not what a failing pass rush looks like.

The pressure numbers back it up. According to Yahoo Sports, the 49ers pressured Prescott 26 times on 55 dropbacks and hit him 12 times. According to Pro Football Focus, Bosa, White and Williams each generated five pressures. A quarterback hit doesn't show up as a sack, but it matters. It forces a hurried throw, knocks the quarterback off his spot, and can set up an incompletion or a turnover on the next play. And there's one play that perfectly captures this. In the first half, the pressure got to Prescott, and he threw a bad pass straight into the chest of Dre Greenlaw. Greenlaw dropped it. If he catches that ball, it's an interception, possibly a pick-six, and the entire game looks different. A dropped interception doesn't show up in a sack total, but it can be the difference between a great day and a frustrating one.

And then there's the play that will be talked about for a long time. With the 49ers leading 38 to 19 in the fourth quarter, Mykel Williams strip-sacked Prescott, and the 49ers recovered. Game over. Except a defensive holding penalty on Lenoir wiped it out. Dallas kept the ball, and went on to score three fourth-quarter touchdowns. John Lynch pointed to that exact play this week. Between "defense in crisis" and "defense in control" sat one yellow flag.

So if the pass rush was winning, why did the defense look so bad by the end? The best explanation is fatigue, and the reason for that fatigue is actually the offense. The 49ers put up 584 yards and 46 points. They averaged 9.4 yards per play. They scored three touchdowns of 60 yards or more, two of them longer than 70 yards. That's great. It's fun to watch, and those are explosive plays you'll take every single week. But there's a cost. When your offense scores in two or three plays, your defense goes right back onto the field without much rest. And Dallas, trying to climb back into the game, sped up the tempo. The Cowboys ran 76 offensive plays. Think about what that means for a defense. Every quick touchdown puts it back on the field. Then the opponent goes faster, which makes it harder to substitute and harder to get the right call in. Tired players make late reads and miss tackles. That's how a strong first half turns into a rough finish.

Shanahan said essentially the same thing on Monday. He said he thought the 49ers definitely rushed the passer better earlier in the game, and that they wore down as the game went on. That's not an excuse. It's an explanation. A defense can be effective for three quarters and still struggle when it's been on the field too long against an offense that won't let it reset.

And here's an important distinction. A pass rush that wins but doesn't finish is a very different problem from a pass rush that never gets close. The first one can be fixed with small things: better finishing, better coordination on stunts, fewer penalties, and a little luck. The second one means you're losing the matchups. The numbers from Dallas put the 49ers firmly in the first group.

None of this means the defense is fine. The penalties were a real problem. The 49ers were flagged 11 times for 100 yards. Bosa had three offside penalties. And Lenoir, playing hurt, was targeted 17 times and allowed three touchdowns, according to Pro Football Focus. Those things have to be cleaned up. But what you're seeing is the outline of a defense that's getting healthier, with key pieces coming back, including a Nick Bosa who might finally be finding his stride.

And I love the mentality. After the game, Bosa said the defense didn't celebrate in the locker room, because it hadn't played well enough. He talked about self-inflicted wounds, penalties and mental errors. And then he said this: feeling like you're the weak link on the team is not a feeling that's going to sit well with this group. That's exactly the right reaction. The defense could have said, "We won, who cares?" Instead, it's angry about its own performance. Bosa was also honest about himself. He admitted he doesn't think he's playing his best football right now, and said that after missing so much time with a torn ACL in 2025 and a calf injury this season, he'd taken some of his fundamentals for granted. He said he's focusing on his leverage, his footwork and his burst off the line, and that he's confident he'll get better.

Shanahan backed him up completely. He said Nick is ready to go and is definitely affecting the quarterback. He said Bosa has a number of plays that look just like Nick, and a few that don't, and that he'll keep improving the more he gets back with the team. And his message was simple: "I'm not worried about Nick." John Lynch went even further, saying Bosa missed training camp and is still adjusting to a new defensive scheme, and predicting that Bosa will start impacting games in a big way this week. That's the first dose of good news: this defense is closer than it looks.

Now let's get to the second piece of good news, and it's a big one. Brock Purdy might be the best quarterback in the NFL against the blitz. And that matters enormously, because the Minnesota Vikings blitz more than anyone in the league, by a lot.

Let's set the scene. Through ten weeks, according to Next Gen Stats, the Vikings have blitzed on 72.6% of opponents' dropbacks, the highest rate through ten weeks since at least 2010. According to Sports Illustrated, that's nearly 30 percentage points higher than the next team, Tampa Bay, and well above the previous high of around 50% for a Flores defense, according to SI. It's an unrelenting mentality. Many defensive coordinators blitz and then back off once they get burned, because sending extra rushers means fewer defenders in coverage, and one big play can make you rethink everything. Flores doesn't back off. He keeps coming no matter what. And it works. Minnesota leads the NFL with 37 sacks and the league's highest pressure rate. It's allowing only 14 points per game, the best mark in football, and 273.3 yards per game, second best. Opposing quarterbacks have a passer rating below 70 against this defense.

Kyle Shanahan clearly respects what he's facing. He said Flores is more unique than all of the other 31 defensive coordinators. He said Flores is the one guy who truly commits to this style over the long term, and that it's not just a fad for a few games. And he described the experience perfectly: in a normal NFL game, the pressure is mostly on third down, but against Brian, it's 70 straight plays of that.

So here's the twist. According to Sports Illustrated, when he's been blitzed this season, Purdy leads the NFL in passing touchdowns, with 12, in passer rating, in yards per attempt and in EPA per dropback. NBC Sports Bay Area lists his passer rating against the blitz at 145.5, the best in the league, ahead of Joe Burrow at 118.7. Different services calculate these numbers a little differently, but every one of them tells the same story: when teams send extra rushers at Purdy, he makes them pay.

For those who aren't familiar, EPA, or expected points added, measures how much a single play changes a team's chances of scoring. A positive number means the play helped the offense. A big positive number against the blitz means Purdy isn't just surviving the pressure. He's producing real value every time the defense gambles. And the sample size is meaningful. This isn't based on a handful of plays. It's built on dozens of blitzed dropbacks across the season.

We saw it again in Dallas. According to Next Gen Stats, Purdy completed all six of his passes against the blitz for 197 yards and two touchdowns. His 66-yard touchdown to Christian McCaffrey came against a blitz with no deep safety. He also completed all four of his throws on scrambles for 135 yards and a touchdown, tied with Patrick Mahomes and Josh Allen for the most scramble passing yards in a game since at least 2018. A quarterback who's already producing against the blitz, with an offense that's shown it can hit explosive plays, is exactly the kind of problem a blitz-heavy defense doesn't want.

Is there a warning sign? Yes, and it's worth being honest about. In Week 6, against Washington defensive coordinator Daronte Jones, who learned from Flores, Purdy faced a blitz on about 41% of his dropbacks and completed only 6 of 11 passes for 86 yards. That's the closest thing he's seen to this Vikings scheme, and it slowed him down. And in his two previous starts against Flores's Minnesota defense, in 2023 and 2024, the 49ers scored exactly 17 points each time and lost both games. In 2024, Purdy was sacked six times. After that game, during the handshakes, he told Flores, "The scheme, man, it's crazy." So this is a real challenge.

But there's also reason to believe this offense is better equipped than ever. According to Sports Illustrated, the 49ers lead the NFL in total offense, in EPA per play and in success rate. They lead the league in EPA per pass, and Purdy leads all quarterbacks at 9.0 yards per attempt. They've allowed only 13 sacks this season, the third fewest in the league, though five of them came in Dallas. And there's another interesting detail. Minnesota's run defense isn't nearly as dominant as its pass defense. According to Sports Illustrated, the Vikings rank 17th in rushing yards allowed per game and 19th in yards per carry. The Lions ran for 230 yards against them, and the Bills ran for 134. That's the kind of weakness Shanahan's running game, with McCaffrey and rookie Kaelon Black, is built to exploit.

McCaffrey has scored a rushing touchdown in eight straight games. Black has 373 rushing yards on 84 carries this season and has impressed Shanahan with his physicality, running over a Cowboys safety on his touchdown and delivering big chip blocks in pass protection. Against a defense that blitzes this often, running backs who can both punish defenders on the ground and pick up blitzers in protection are worth their weight in gold. If the 49ers can run the ball on first down, they stay out of the long-yardage situations where Flores is most dangerous.

There's one more number that tells you how important a fast start will be. Linebacker Blake Cashman said this week that Minnesota's defense is most dangerous when it has a lead, because that's when it can unleash its full set of maximum-pressure looks. In other words, if the 49ers fall behind early and become predictable, Flores gets to call the game on his terms. If San Francisco can score early and stay balanced, the Vikings can't simply pin their ears back on every snap.

And history under Shanahan backs that up. According to Sports Illustrated, the 49ers are 16 and 46 under Shanahan when trailing at halftime, including the playoffs. That's not unusual for an NFL team, because coming from behind is hard for everyone. But against a defense that becomes even more aggressive with a lead, the first half of Sunday's game might matter more than ever. A fast start, with an early touchdown or two, would force Minnesota to play a more balanced game and give the 49ers' running attack a chance to settle in.

And that's the second dose of good news: the 49ers have the best quarterback in the league against exactly the kind of defense they're about to face.

Now let's talk about the third piece of good news: the receiver room. Go back to October 25th, when the 49ers suffered their only loss of the season, against the Atlanta Falcons. That day, the 49ers were extremely short at wide receiver, with several of their top receivers unavailable. The group they had on the field was thin and banged up, and it showed. The 49ers turned the ball over four times and lost 29 to 20.

Now look at the room heading into Mexico City. Mike Evans, who has missed the last three games with Achilles tendinitis, returned to practice on Thursday. Cam Inman of the Bay Area News Group wrote that Evans looked unhindered in his first practice back. He was limited again on Friday, and he's officially questionable for Sunday night, with the team planning to make a final decision on Saturday. Shanahan said Evans had a good practice, was a little sore, but is going in the right direction, and that it would be great to get him back. In six games this season, Evans has 27 catches for 420 yards and three touchdowns.

Keenan Allen, acquired from the Colts at the trade deadline for a 2027 sixth-round pick, caught five passes for 61 yards in his debut in Dallas, with four of those catches going for first downs. Deebo Samuel came back against the Cowboys and scored twice, including the 70-yard reverse. Jordan Watkins has emerged as a real contributor, with his first NFL touchdown against the Raiders. Demarcus Robinson, back from injured reserve, is the kind of reliable veteran every receiver room needs. And George Kittle and Christian McCaffrey remain two of the best pass catchers at their positions in football.

And then there's the most encouraging sign of all: rookie receiver De'Zhaun Stribling. Stribling suffered an ankle injury in the season opener against the Rams in Australia and needed surgery on the deltoid ligament, a ligament that helps stabilize the inside of the ankle. It's a significant injury with a long recovery. But this week in Colorado Springs, according to 49ers reporter David Lombardi, Stribling worked on the side field in front of the media for the first time, doing resistance work with a weighted sled. The 49ers hope he can return later this season, with Shanahan previously pointing toward December. Seeing him do any work at all is meaningful. It means his rehab is moving forward.

Stribling had an excellent training camp, and plenty of people believed he could be a real contributor as a rookie. He's a big, physical receiver with a wide catch radius, a willing blocker and the kind of player who can win contested catches. If he returns in December, the 49ers could end the season with a receiver room that looks completely different from the one that took the field against Atlanta. Veterans, a returning star, a trade acquisition and a promising rookie. That kind of depth gives an offense more ways to attack when injuries hit. And that's the third dose of good news.

There's still some uncertainty around the edges of the roster, too. Keenan Allen faces a possible suspension of at least three games, according to ESPN's Adam Schefter, with the timing still unknown. That's one reason Evans's return, and eventually Stribling's, matter so much. Depth protects you when the unexpected happens. On defense, cornerback Nate Hobbs and defensive tackle C.J. West are both still on injured reserve. Both practiced fully this week and are listed as questionable, and the 49ers have until Tuesday to decide on Hobbs. Shanahan has said Hobbs has looked good for the last two weeks and is chomping at the bit to get back. He's also reminded everyone that you have to cut someone to activate someone. And there's one more deadline: Brandon Aiyuk's roster exemption expires on November 23rd, the day after the game, and a release is widely expected. So the next few days could bring several important roster moves.

Meanwhile, Renardo Green, who left the Dallas game with back tightness, practiced fully on Thursday and Friday and has no injury designation, which means he's expected to play. That's important, because he's likely to see a lot of Justin Jefferson. Dre Greenlaw, who missed Wednesday with a neck issue, practiced on a limited basis on Thursday and Friday and is questionable. Shanahan said the team is hoping that continues to go in the right direction.

Now let's talk about the setting, because in a game played this high above sea level, logistics can matter almost as much as the game plan. Mexico City sits more than 7,000 feet above sea level. That's a dramatic change from Santa Clara, which is barely above sea level. So after beating Dallas, the 49ers flew straight to Colorado Springs, where they've been practicing all week at the Air Force Academy, at roughly 7,258 feet. The stadium in Mexico City sits at about 7,352 feet. The Vikings, by contrast, are training in an altitude chamber at their facility in Minnesota and arriving in Mexico City on Saturday, the day before the game.

The logic of arriving early is simple. The body needs days, not hours, to adjust to a big change in elevation. A team that arrives right before a game is asking its players to perform while their bodies are still adjusting. The 49ers decided that the cost of leaving early is worth the benefit. According to exercise physiologists interviewed by Yahoo Sports, the science mostly supports them. Dr. Benjamin Levine said a few days of real acclimatization helps everything, including endurance and sleep. Another expert called chamber training better than nothing, but not a full adjustment. To be fair, one expert also warned that a long road trip can create its own kind of fatigue, because players are away from their families and routines.

And the 49ers have already seen this approach work this season. In Week 1, they traveled to Melbourne, Australia, and arrived early to adjust to the enormous time difference. The Rams arrived much later. San Francisco won 27 to 7. In 2022, the 49ers also prepared in Colorado Springs before beating the Cardinals 38 to 10 in Mexico City. So they have every reason to trust the plan. Anyone who's ever tried to run at elevation in Colorado knows how quickly you get winded. Shanahan admitted he was out of breath just walking to his press conference on Wednesday. The 49ers have been dealing with that feeling for a full week. The Vikings will feel it for the first time on Saturday.

Now, the Vikings are a very different team than they were a month ago. Before trading for Kirk Cousins, Minnesota's offense was holding the team back. In the loss to Buffalo, Carson Wentz went 8 of 25 for 81 yards with two interceptions in a 23 to 6 defeat. Then Cousins arrived, and the Vikings beat Green Bay 30 to 10 at Lambeau Field, with their defense sacking Jordan Love eight times and forcing four turnovers. The offense doesn't need to be spectacular. It just needs to stop giving the game away. And with Cousins and Justin Jefferson, it can do that.

But the 49ers have a recent blueprint. Just two weeks ago, when Cousins was with the Raiders, the 49ers forced five second-half takeaways against Las Vegas, and Cousins threw three interceptions. Rookie linebacker Jaden Dugger returned one 35 yards for a touchdown. That's proof that this defense can make Cousins uncomfortable. Shanahan also pointed out this week that, in Cousins's return at Green Bay, Minnesota's defense created turnovers and short fields that earned him about 17 points. In other words, the Vikings' offense is at its best when its defense hands it easy opportunities. Take those away, and Cousins has to drive the length of the field against a 49ers defense that knows him very well. On Sunday, Minnesota will be without receiver Jordan Addison, who's been ruled out with a thumb injury. Former 49ers receiver Jauan Jennings, who practiced fully all week, could be a bigger part of the game plan.

There's one more connection worth mentioning: the 49ers' offense and defense are linked. The way the offense scores affects how much the defense has to play. A quick-strike offense is a gift, but it also means the defense has to stay ready to go back out quickly. Against Minnesota, the ideal plan might be a little different from Dallas: a few more sustained drives, more time of possession, and a defense that gets to rest on the sideline. Long drives would also help against the altitude, wearing down a Vikings defense that's already planning to rotate heavily. If the 49ers can find that balance, using big plays when they're there without wearing out their own defenders, they'll be in great shape for the rest of the season.

So here's the picture heading into Mexico City. The defense, by the numbers that matter, is better than the score in Dallas suggested, and the players' mindset is exactly right. The offense is explosive, and its quarterback has the best numbers in the league against the kind of defense he's about to face. The receiver room is getting healthier, with Evans questionable and Stribling back on the field. And the travel plan is built specifically around the altitude. That's the triple dose of good news, and it's the reason so many people around this team feel confident heading into the biggest regular season game of the year.

But let's be honest. This is still the toughest test of the season. Flores is five and oh against Shanahan's offenses, and his defense might be the best in the league. Nothing is guaranteed on Sunday night, and anyone who tells you otherwise isn't being honest. A win would move the 49ers to 9 and 1, give them a two-game lead over Minnesota and the head-to-head tiebreaker in the race for the NFC's top seed. A loss would make the top of the conference a crowded fight between the 49ers, Vikings, Lions and Bears.
