Now let's talk about that blitz, because this is the matchup that's keeping offensive coordinators awake at night. Through ten weeks, according to Next Gen Stats, the Vikings have blitzed on 72.6% of opponents' dropbacks. Different stat services measure it slightly differently. By one count, Minnesota has rushed five or more players on 68.9% of passing plays, and the next-closest team, the New York Giants, is at 40.3%. Either way, Minnesota is in a category of its own. The league average is around 32%, already one of the highest rates in years. The Vikings lead the NFL with 37 sacks, a 35% pressure rate and a 27.7% quarterback hit rate. They also have ten interceptions, fourth most in the league.

And here's the part that should catch your attention. The last time these two teams met, in Week 2 of 2024, the Vikings were blitzing around 40% of the time early in that season, according to CBS Sports. That was already enough to ruin San Francisco's afternoon. Purdy completed 28 of 36 passes for 294 yards, but he was sacked six times and threw an interception, and the 49ers lost 23 to 17 in Minnesota. After the game, during the handshakes, Purdy told Flores, "The scheme, man, it's crazy." That was a respectful compliment from a quarterback who had just been chased all afternoon. Now imagine that same scheme with the blitz rate pushed up by more than 30 percentage points.

But here's where the story gets interesting, because the 49ers' offense might be the best in the league at beating the blitz. According to NBC Sports Bay Area, Purdy has a passer rating of 145.5 when blitzed this season, the best mark in the NFL. Joe Burrow is a distant second at 118.7. Against Dallas, according to Next Gen Stats, Purdy completed all six of his passes against the blitz for 197 yards and two touchdowns. His 66-yard touchdown to Christian McCaffrey in the fourth quarter came against a blitz with no deep safety behind it.

And there's another stat that makes this matchup fascinating. According to analysis from Next Gen Stats, the 49ers send all five eligible receivers into their passing concepts on 80.1% of their dropbacks, when you include chip blocks. That's the highest rate in the league. In other words, most teams that see a heavy blitz coming keep extra blockers at home. The 49ers do the opposite. They send everybody out. That's a true immovable-object-meets-unstoppable-force situation. One side sends extra rushers. The other side sends extra receivers. Something has to give.

So how do the 49ers make it work? The philosophy is surprisingly simple. Sometimes, they're willing to accept that a rusher will come free. The trade-off is that someone downfield is going to be open, especially against a defense that, oddly enough, mixes in a lot of zone coverage even while blitzing aggressively. That combination is one of the most unusual things in football: a team that sends pressure from everywhere, then drops the rest of its defenders into the soft spots. If the quarterback can get the ball out before the pressure arrives, or escape it, there's real yardage available.

And that's where Brock Purdy becomes a cheat code. His ability to escape, scramble and keep his eyes downfield is exactly what you want against a defense like this. Against Dallas, according to Next Gen Stats, he completed all four of his passes on scrambles for 135 yards and a touchdown, tied for the most scramble passing yards in a game since at least 2018, alongside Patrick Mahomes and Josh Allen. Most quarterbacks fall apart once the pocket collapses. Purdy seems to get better the longer the play goes on. And it's not luck. It's preparation, instincts, and teammates who know exactly where to go when the play breaks down. Christian McCaffrey and George Kittle have a great feel for the scramble drill. McCaffrey's 74-yard touchdown in Dallas started with him staying in to block, then leaking out once Purdy escaped. That's the kind of chemistry that can turn Flores's aggression into big plays for San Francisco.

Now imagine adding Mike Evans to that picture. Evans has always been one of the best contested-catch receivers in football. Against a defense that blitzes this often, there are fewer defenders in coverage, which often means one-on-one matchups on the outside. A 6-foot-5 receiver who wins those battles is the perfect answer. With Evans, Keenan Allen, Deebo Samuel, Kittle and McCaffrey all available, Flores would have to choose who to leave open. That's the dream scenario for Shanahan.

There's also a role for the running backs in protection. Rookie Kaelon Black has earned praise from Shanahan for his physicality, including running over a Cowboys safety on his touchdown run and delivering a big chip block on defensive tackle Quinnen Williams. Against Minnesota, the running backs' ability to recognize and pick up blitzers will be crucial. One missed assignment can turn into a sack or a turnover. One great pickup can turn into a long touchdown.

Now let's talk about the setting, because the 49ers chose it very deliberately. They're spending the week at the Air Force Academy in Colorado Springs, which sits at roughly 7,258 feet above sea level. The stadium in Mexico City, the venue long known as Estadio Azteca and now officially Estadio Banorte, sits at about 7,350 feet. So the 49ers are practicing at almost exactly the same elevation as the game. They flew straight there from Dallas after Sunday's win, and they'll travel to Mexico City on Saturday.

This isn't the first time the 49ers have done this. Back in 2022, before their Monday night game against the Arizona Cardinals in Mexico City, they also prepared in Colorado Springs, and they won 38 to 10. The New England Patriots used the same approach in 2017, training in Colorado Springs before beating the Raiders 33 to 8 in Mexico City. So there's a track record.

The Vikings are taking a very different route. They're staying home in Minnesota for most of the week and flying in on Saturday. Instead of traveling early, they built an altitude chamber at their facility in Eagan, capable of simulating conditions up to 12,000 feet. According to the team's vice president of player health and performance, Tyler Williams, nearly the entire roster has done at least five sessions in it, with close to 450 sessions logged overall.

So which approach is better? According to a Yahoo Sports report that spoke to exercise physiologists, the experts mostly favor the 49ers' approach. Dr. Benjamin Levine said that having a few days of real acclimatization helps everything, including endurance and sleep. Grégoire Millet described chamber training as better than nothing, but not a full adjustment. Another expert, Laura Lewis, pointed out that the 49ers have also had a full week to get used to how the ball travels in thin air, and suggested the Vikings might notice a difference during warmups. To be fair, one physiologist, Andrew Subudhi, also warned that a long road trip, with players away from home and their families, can create its own kind of fatigue. And he suggested that the 49ers' belief in their approach might matter as much as the physiology itself.

The players clearly respect the challenge. Trent Williams, who has seen just about everything this league can throw at a player, talked this week about the challenges of playing at altitude. The message was simple: you can't fake your way through it. You can be the toughest guy in the building and work harder than anyone, but there's real science behind it, and you have to respect it. That's old-school wisdom from one of the best offensive linemen of his generation, and at 38, he knows better than anyone how to manage his body over a long week.

Shanahan, as always, kept it in perspective. He said altitude is rarely the difference between winning and losing, but that anything that helps is worth doing. He compared it to playing in Denver, joking that he always thought the Broncos had about a 2% advantage at home, before admitting he was making that number up. His point was simple: it's nicer to play when you're used to it, and you're not as out of breath.

Now let's step into the time machine, because this game is a full-circle story for Brock Purdy. The last time the 49ers played in Mexico City was that 2022 Monday night game against Arizona. Who was a rookie on that team? Brock Purdy, the final pick in the 2022 draft, Mr. Irrelevant himself. He wasn't the starter yet. That would come a few weeks later, after Jimmy Garoppolo broke his foot. But with the game well in hand, Shanahan pulled his starters, and Purdy got some snaps in only his third professional game. And on one play, he did something that has lived on the internet ever since. He turned and handed the ball off the wrong way. On the broadcast, Joe Buck noticed that the ball had been handed the wrong way, and Troy Aikman joked about it. It was a small, funny rookie mistake, the kind every young quarterback makes. But nobody could have imagined that the same quarterback would return to Mexico City four years later as a leading MVP candidate.

Think about how far he's come since that night. A few weeks after that game, Purdy replaced the injured Garoppolo and led the 49ers to the NFC Championship Game as a rookie. He finished fourth in MVP voting in 2023 and led the team to a Super Bowl. And now, in 2026, he has 20 touchdown passes and just five interceptions, the best career passer rating among active quarterbacks, and a coach who says he's giving him the best quarterback play he's ever coached, ahead of even Matt Ryan's 2016 MVP season. Going from a wrong-way handoff in garbage time to an MVP candidate under the Sunday night lights is one of the best stories in the league.

And here's the best part. The other rookie involved in that moment was running back Jordan Mason, who was part of the mix-up. Mason later became a key part of the 49ers' running game before being traded to Minnesota. So the wrong-way handoff duo is heading back to Mexico City, on opposite sidelines this time. You can't make this stuff up. Football has a funny way of bringing old teammates back together.

So let's put it all together. Here's what the 49ers need to do on Sunday night.

First, they have to stay out of second-and-long and third-and-long. Flores's defense is built to put you behind the chains, stack up negative runs and force the quarterback to hold the ball until the pressure arrives. The 49ers convert a league-high 52% of their third downs, but they also reach third down less often than almost any team, because they keep picking up big gains on early downs. That's the formula. Run effectively on first down with McCaffrey and Kaelon Black, use play-action and quick throws, and never let the defense dictate the situation.

Second, they have to be aggressive. Against a defense like this, playing scared is a slow death. If you keep extra blockers in on every play and throw only checkdowns, the Vikings will eventually win the down. The 49ers' identity is to attack: five receivers in the route, quick decisions, and a quarterback who's comfortable making plays when the pocket breaks down. Purdy's 145.5 passer rating against the blitz says that identity works.

Remember, the 49ers lead the NFL in total offense, at almost 400 yards per game, and in yards per play. They're second in scoring at 31.2 points per game. Minnesota has faced only one offense this season that ranks in the top five in efficiency, according to some analysts. So in a sense, both units are about to face the best test they've seen all year.

Third, they have to protect the football. In the two games against Flores's Minnesota defense in 2023 and 2024, the 49ers turned the ball over five times, and Purdy was responsible for four of those, according to Sharp Football Analysis. Turnovers are how pressure wins even when it doesn't produce sacks.

Think back to October 2023, on Monday Night Football in Minnesota. The Vikings beat the 49ers 22 to 17, and Purdy threw two interceptions in the final six minutes with San Francisco trailing by five, including a late pick by Camryn Bynum that sealed it. That game was a perfect example of how Flores's pressure works. It doesn't always sack you. It speeds up your clock, forces you to throw a little early or a little late, and waits for the mistake. Avoiding that kind of ending is the single most important thing Purdy can do on Sunday night.

Fourth, the defense has to be cleaner than it was in Dallas. That means fewer penalties, after being flagged 11 times for 100 yards against the Cowboys. It means better communication against Jefferson and Jennings. Jefferson had nine catches against Green Bay last week, his season high, and Cousins clearly looks for him early and often. Lenoir was targeted 17 times in Dallas and allowed three touchdowns, according to Pro Football Focus. Whether it's Lenoir, Green or, if he's activated, Nate Hobbs lining up across from Jefferson, the 49ers cannot afford the busted coverages that gave Dallas free touchdowns. One blown assignment against Jefferson, and he's gone. The safeties, Malik Mustapha and Ji'Ayir Brown, will need to be on the same page with the corners on every snap, especially on deep shots when the Vikings try to take advantage of the thin air. And it means finishing the pressure. The 49ers pressured Dak Prescott 26 times on 55 dropbacks, according to Yahoo Sports, but recorded only one shared sack. Nick Bosa still doesn't have a sack this season. Shanahan said he's not worried about Nick and that Bosa is definitely affecting the quarterback. John Lynch went further, predicting that Bosa would start impacting games in a big way this week. This would be a very good time for that prediction to come true.

And fifth, they have to handle the moment. This is the kind of game that tells you who you really are. A win would move the 49ers to 9 and 1, two games ahead of Minnesota, with the head-to-head tiebreaker in the race for the NFC's top seed. A loss would open the door for the Vikings, Lions and Bears, all of whom are at 7 and 2.

And it's not just the top seed. In the NFC West, the Seahawks and Rams are both at 6 and 4, two and a half games behind San Francisco. A win keeps that cushion comfortable heading into the stretch run, with Seattle coming to Levi's Stadium on November 30th. A loss would make the division race a little tighter. Every game from here on carries real weight.

Here's my honest take. The 73% blitz number is real, and it's scary. Flores has a perfect record against Shanahan's offenses, and his defense might be the best in the league. But this 49ers offense is built differently from the one Flores beat in 2023 and 2024. Purdy is better against the blitz than any quarterback in football. The 49ers lead the league in total offense and yards per play. They've spent a full week at altitude. And they might get Mike Evans back. If there was ever a time to break the Flores curse, it's this one. And if they do it on the biggest stage of the season, in front of their Mexican fans, it will be a statement the rest of the NFC can't ignore.
