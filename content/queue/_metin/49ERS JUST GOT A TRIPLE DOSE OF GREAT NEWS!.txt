Let's start with the reaction to the Keenan Allen trade, because the national media had a lot to say. On The Rich Eisen Show, the hosts reacted as the news broke, and the takeaway was simple: great move. They pointed out that the 49ers got in ahead of everyone, landing one of the best route runners of his generation while other teams that need receivers, like Kansas City, were still shopping. And then they started picturing it. Purdy under center. McCaffrey behind him. Kittle on the line. Evans on the outside when he's healthy. Allen in the slot. Deebo floating all over the formation. Trent Williams protecting the blind side. One of them summed it up perfectly: that lineup sounds like a nightmare for opposing defenses.

And the numbers raise an even crazier possibility: the 49ers might have been the team nobody wanted to face even before the trade. In the latest power rankings, ESPN has them at number two behind Buffalo. Pete Prisco of CBS Sports also has them second, writing that they just keep playing as well as anybody in the NFC, despite their many injuries, and noting that Brock Purdy is in the MVP conversation. If Fred Warner, Nick Bosa, Mike Evans, McCaffrey and Trent Williams are healthy for a playoff game, a lot of people would take the 49ers over almost anybody in the NFL.

That's the big "if," of course. And that brings us to Wednesday's injury report, and the triple dose of good news.

Good news number one: Deebo Samuel is back at practice. After missing the last three games with a hamstring injury, Deebo was a limited participant on Wednesday. And according to Matt Lively, Deebo expects to play on Sunday in Dallas. Before he got hurt against Seattle on October 11th, he had 16 catches for 206 yards and three touchdowns, plus 78 rushing yards, in five games. He's the kind of player who changes how a defense has to line up before the ball is even snapped. Defensive coordinators have to know where Deebo is on every single play, because he can hurt you from anywhere. He can be a receiver, a running back, a decoy or a gadget weapon, sometimes all on the same drive. Getting him back changes everything for Shanahan.

And it's worth remembering just how special Deebo can be when he's healthy. In 2021, he was a first-team All-Pro, piling up more than 1,400 receiving yards while also running for eight touchdowns out of the backfield. He became the template for the "wide back," a receiver who can line up anywhere and break tackles like a running back. He's been a core part of this offense for years, through Super Bowl runs and deep playoff trips. And he gives Shanahan something nobody else on the roster can: a player who can turn a two-yard screen into a 40-yard gain just by refusing to go down.

The staff will probably be careful with him this week. Hamstrings are tricky, and Deebo has been out for a month. A limited Wednesday is a good first step. If he goes full on Thursday or Friday, that's the strongest sign he'll play. And even on a snap count, Deebo changes the way defenses line up.

Good news number two: Keenan Allen's first practice. Allen had only about two hours of installation before his first practice with the team on Wednesday. Shanahan said the 49ers were excited to add him and that he makes them a lot better. He praised Allen's ability to separate, his hands, his blocking and his work ethic. And Shanahan said the staff will decide on Allen's role for Sunday based on how he looks by Saturday. So there's a real chance we see him in Dallas, even if it's a limited package at first.

And if you're newer to the Faithful and wondering who exactly Allen is, here's the short version. He's 34, a 14-year veteran out of Cal, and a third-round pick of the Chargers in 2013. He's made six Pro Bowls. He won Comeback Player of the Year in 2017. He caught 108 passes for 1,243 yards in 2023. And in October 2025, he became the fastest player in NFL history to reach 1,000 career catches, doing it in 159 games. He has 1,095 career receptions and more than 12,400 receiving yards. This season, in eight games with the Colts, he had 40 catches for 351 yards and a touchdown, leading Indianapolis in receptions. He isn't the explosive player he was in his prime, and his 8.8 yards per catch is a career low. But he's still one of the smartest route runners in football, and he's a one-year rental who costs the 49ers almost nothing against the cap.

Shanahan also made it clear that both trade additions, Allen and DeForest Buckner, really help this team. That's a big statement from a coach who, just last week, admitted it's very hard for a new receiver to learn his offense in the middle of the season. The fact that he's already talking about Allen's role for this week tells you how quickly he expects Allen to pick things up.

But Shanahan was also honest about the suspension question. Allen is facing league discipline after being charged with two misdemeanors related to drunk driving in Indianapolis before the season. Adam Schefter has reported that he faces at least a three-game suspension. On Wednesday, Shanahan said the team is waiting on the league, and that the timing could go either way, meaning it's still possible the suspension comes this season. So that cloud hasn't gone away. The 49ers are enjoying Allen while they have him, and they'll adjust if the league acts.

Now, the not-so-good news. Renardo Green, with his back injury, and Deommodore Lenoir, with his foot injury, both missed practice. Earlier in the week, both were called day-to-day. Now let's talk about what makes this offense so dangerous when it gets healthy. Because one of the biggest advantages Kyle Shanahan has is personnel flexibility.

The idea is simple, even if the execution is complicated. When an offense has two big players on the field, whether that's two tight ends, or a tight end and a fullback, the defense has to respect the run. With three wide receivers and one running back, a defense can call certain fronts and coverages that make running the ball very hard. But with two bigs, the offense can dictate. The run stays a credible threat, even on downs when the defense expects a pass. That's the heart of Shanahan's system. It's why his offenses have been so hard to defend for so many years, no matter who's been catching the ball.

Now put yourself on the other sideline. McCaffrey is in the backfield. Kittle is attached to the line. Kyle Juszczyk comes into the game at fullback. Do you keep an extra defensive back on the field to help cover Allen? Fine. Now that smaller defender might have to take on a lead blocker, with McCaffrey coming straight at him. Bring in a linebacker instead, and watch what happens when the fullback motions out. Suddenly the offense spreads out. Kittle can release up the seam. And the linebacker who came in to stop the run now has to cover in space.

That's the nightmare for a defensive coordinator, and it's exactly why Shanahan loves personnel groupings with two bigs. The offense can change the picture without changing the people on the field. And now Shanahan adds a receiver who uses his release and the timing of his break to punish any defender who hesitates for even a split second. Then imagine Mike Evans healthy on the outside, and Deebo available to move across the formation. Who gets your extra defender? Go ahead, pick one. Now tell me who you're leaving alone.

New players also generate new ideas. When the 49ers traded for McCaffrey in 2022, Shanahan's playbook expanded, because McCaffrey could do things previous running backs couldn't. Deebo has always given the staff unique ways to get creative. Kittle does too. And Allen, with his route-running and his feel for zone coverage, adds another layer. It's not about stealing a magic playbook. It's about matching what your players do best with what the defense is giving you.

And remember, the 49ers already lead the NFL with a 52.7 percent offensive success rate, and they're the only team with a rushing success rate above 50 percent. They rank first in DVOA. They've done that with a receiver room that has been decimated by injuries for weeks. Now imagine those numbers with Allen and Deebo in the lineup.

When a quarterback is throwing to receivers who are still learning the playbook, like Allen this week, the protection has to buy him an extra beat.
